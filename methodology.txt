It rests on one documented fact: modern hiring screens rank candidates. They do not reject them. A ranking screen scores every candidate against the role and returns an ordered list. Recruiters contact people from the top down. Anyone below the point where they stop receives nothing — not a rejection, because no rejection decision was ever made. That is the mechanical definition of application silence. This isn&#x27;t an inference. The companies that build these screens describe them this way in their own product documentation: Sourcing platforms return ranked lists with fit percentages and match labels, not accept-or-reject decisions. Inbound applications are now scored against the job the moment they arrive, so they reach the recruiter already ranked. Ranking before a human has spread beyond enterprise software to small recruiting teams, including applications that arrive as email attachments. One vendor, describing the strong candidate ranked far down its own list, puts it plainly: They are simply never reached. Two corrections to how this is usually told. First, “robots reject you” is wrong. At least one vendor scores every application and explicitly refuses to auto-reject on that score; a person still makes the decision. “Ranked and never reached” is the accurate version. Second, the triage predates the software. Recruiters have always read the first batch closely, skimmed the next, and judged the rest on a job title. The software formalises that. It didn&#x27;t invent it. What you get back A score band, and a split between two causes: The Filter Recruiters can&#x27;t find or place you. Your target title and the language on your résumé don&#x27;t match what they search for. The Fit You&#x27;re found, but your proof doesn&#x27;t hold up in a fast read. Strong in places, a list of duties in others. The split matters because the fixes are different. A Filter problem is solved at the top of your résumé. A Fit problem is solved in the evidence underneath it. The Autopsy reads your experience, not your identity, so the findings are identical either way. The eight causes of silence The Autopsy can name eight causes of application silence. Each is derived from what screening platforms document that they read, not from generic résumé advice. Several can&#x27;t be fixed by editing your résumé at all, and the verdict will tell you when that&#x27;s the case. Cause What it means, and what the screen does Fixable on the page? Achievement legibility Your impact and specialisation are missing or unreadable in a fast pass. The screen: sourcing platforms filter on high-signal evidence of impact and achievement, not keyword counts. Yes on the page Professional footprint Evidence that lives off your résumé — code, publications, certifications, talks — is missing or can&#x27;t be found. The screen: platforms index technical forums, code repositories and academic publications alongside the résumé. Partly fixed off the page Trajectory and internal displacement Your career path scores poorly on likelihood to move, or an internal employee ranks above you on the same system. The screen: talent platforms model career progression and rank external applicants against current employees on one graph. Rarely Title lineage and seniority Your title history doesn&#x27;t map to the progression the role expects. The screen: ranking reads title sequence and seniority, not just the most recent title. Often Role economics and geography The role was never realistically available at your pay level or location. The screen: staffing providers publish cost comparisons for placing roles in lower-cost markets. No Channel mismatch You&#x27;re applying through a channel that can&#x27;t read your evidence well. The screen: job boards, in-house teams and agencies evaluate on different economics. Yes by changing channel Target mismatch Your evidence is clear, but consistently aimed at roles whose scope or seniority it doesn&#x27;t reach. The screen: ranking scores fit to the specific role, so strong evidence aimed wrong still ranks low. The screen: recruiting vendors tell hiring teams outright to prioritise passive candidates over applicants. No become findable instead How confident each finding is For every cause, the mechanism is documented. How often each one is the reason for silence across the market is not — no reliable source measures that. So the Autopsy tells you which cause your evidence points to, and how strongly. It will never tell you that a cause affects some percentage of all job seekers. Two causes carry extra rules Target mismatch can only be diagnosed as a pattern across several job descriptions. On a single posting it is only ever flagged as low-confidence and secondary, because one posting may be a deliberate stretch. The finding comes first; only then does the Autopsy ask whether a career change is intentional. Your intent shapes the advice. It never changes the finding. Role economics is reported analytically. Where a role was never economically open to you, the useful thing is to know it, so you stop spending applications on it. The Recruiter-Fit Matrix The Recruiter-Fit Matrix scores eight dimensions from 1–5, for a maximum of 40 points. It shows where your résumé stands now and where a realistic rewrite could take it. Why a matrix and not a match rate A keyword match rate tells you how many words overlap with a posting. It says nothing about whether a recruiter reading for eight seconds would see a candidate worth calling. The Matrix scores whether your résumé is aligned, searchable, evidenced, clear and ready to present. The eight dimensions # Dimension What it measures 1 Role alignment Whether the title and summary match the target role within the first five words. 2 Keyword density Whether recruiter Boolean-search terms appear in the top third of the résumé. 3 Evidence of results Whether claims are supported by numbers, names, outcomes or other proof. 4 Positioning clarity Whether a recruiter can describe the candidate&#x27;s value in one sentence. 5 Seniority signal Whether the candidate&#x27;s level is clear—neither understated nor inflated. 6 Sector credibility Whether the background, terminology and presentation fit the target sector. 7 Recency weighting Whether the most recent role is the most detailed and evidence-rich. 8 Presentability Whether a recruiter could forward the résumé to a client without editing it. Before, After and Delta Before: your current résumé score. After: the realistic score after the Truth Layer rewrite. Delta: the improvement, calculated as After minus Before. Every dimension score is a whole number from 1–5. The After score is not automatically set to 5: missing evidence or proof points can limit the realistic result. Score bands 36–40 Shortlist-Ready 30–35 Strong but with named gaps 24–29 Needs rewrite to be presented Below 24 Not yet ready—proof points required from the candidate What the projection means The band is based on the After score. That score is a realistic projection of the same résumé with the recommended changes applied, not a measured hiring outcome. It shows how much of the gap can be addressed on the page; it cannot predict what an employer will do. Where applications die The Autopsy estimates how your silence splits across three stages, because each one fails for a different reason and has a different fix. Stage What happens Fix The screen Your application is ranked below the point where recruiters stop reading. No person decides anything. The top of your résumé: target title, the language recruiters search for, one hard proof point. The recruiter pass A person reads it and doesn&#x27;t shortlist you, usually because the proof isn&#x27;t scannable in a fast read. The evidence under the top section, rewritten from duties into results. Postings that were never hiring The role wasn&#x27;t a genuine opening. None on your side. The fix is not spending applications there. The third stage is real and documented. The Congressional Research Service reported in April 2025 that staffing agencies may post openings to show employers they can attract strong candidates, a sales device aimed at winning the client. Agencies in specialist fields also post to monitor candidate availability and salary expectations. Neither is aimed at exploiting candidates, but neither leads to a hire. What the stage split is and isn&#x27;t. The percentages in your Autopsy are an estimate for your applications. They are not a market-wide rate. No survey breaks never-hiring postings out by channel, and a market-wide figure wouldn&#x27;t describe your applications anyway. Why it matters where you applied Silence from a job board, an in-house recruiter and a specialist agency are three different failures, because each channel is paid differently and evaluates you differently. The Autopsy asks where your applications went for this reason. Channel Paid by Relationship with you Incentive to give feedback Who looks first Specialist recruiter The client, on placement Multi-year, across searches Mixed: real relationship value, limited by time A person In-house recruiter One employer, on salary One role at a time Weak A person, eventually Job board Employers, on engagement None Close to none at volume Often software; sometimes no one Job boards earn their revenue from employers — pay-per-click, sponsored placement, access to résumé databases — not from matching you to a job. Recruit Holdings&#x27; HR Technology segment, which includes Indeed and Glassdoor, reported $7.38 billion in revenue for fiscal 2024. The model rewards volume on both sides. In-house recruiters handle many roles at once alongside planning and coordination work. Feedback practice across employers is poor and well measured. Talent Board&#x27;s 2018 North American benchmark found just 19.5% of employers ask interviewed-but-unselected candidates for feedback, and a 25.5-point gap between how employers rated their rejection experience and how candidates rated it. Specialist recruiters earn mostly on placement, often with a 60 to 90 day guarantee period that keeps them engaged after a hire. They depend on a candidate pool they return to across searches, which rewards closing the loop. But they run several paid searches at once, which rations their time. The honest limit No research breaks feedback practice down by recruiter channel. The employer-wide figures above are the best available proxy for in-house behaviour, not a direct measurement of it. And no channel is exempt from ranking software: agency marketplaces now grade submissions too. The specialist channel is cleaner, not untouched. What the score does not claim The Silence Score is confident about how screens work and probabilistic about your specific case. Here is exactly where the line sits. It doesn&#x27;t know any employer&#x27;s settings. We know what screening platforms document that they read. We don&#x27;t know how a particular company has configured one. Every finding comes with a confidence level for that reason. It doesn&#x27;t quote rates it can&#x27;t source. You won&#x27;t find the claim here that most jobs are never posted, or that nearly all applications never reach a person. We looked for the sources behind those numbers. They don&#x27;t hold up, so we don&#x27;t use them. It doesn&#x27;t treat vendor marketing as fact. Screening companies publish performance claims: faster hiring, more replies. Those are unaudited. We cite vendors for what their products do, never for how well they do it. It doesn&#x27;t promise an outcome. The Autopsy tells you why your applications are going silent and what to change. Whether you get an interview depends on employers and a labour market we don&#x27;t control. It doesn&#x27;t have outcome data yet. When we publish results, each figure will state who it covers, the period, where the data came from, and its weaknesses. It will tell you when the problem isn&#x27;t your résumé. Several causes of silence can&#x27;t be fixed by rewriting anything. A diagnosis that only ever recommends a rewrite isn&#x27;t a diagnosis. It will return an unwelcome answer. If the evidence says you&#x27;re aiming at roles you don&#x27;t yet reach, the verdict says so, first, and before asking whether that&#x27;s deliberate.
